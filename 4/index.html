    RS422 and 485

    In previous notes, we saw that despite the simplicity and ubiquity of RS232. It does have some major drawbacks.

    • Its range is limited
    • Its bandwidth is limited and related to range
    • It is point to point, one duplex connection per link.

    Other physical protocols have been developed to eliminate these issues and are commonly used in industrial communications. In data centers, we will find them in the automation of Power and Heating, Ventilation and Cooling/Air Conditioning (HVAC).

    For example, RS422 can transmit to up to 10 receivers and can communicate at up to 10 Mb/s. It uses balanced, differential communications which provides much better noise immunity than RS232. RS422 can be used for long range point-to-point links, as a replacement for RS232. It should also be used where a single MASTER device will transmit data to (up to) ten SLAVE devices. If you examine a boat which is wired for traditional electronic instruments, you will see the use of a communications standard NMEA0183 and the physical standard RS422.

    RS422 should not be used where bi-directional data is required between multiple devices; we use RS485 for this functionality. Process control systems have used this protocol for many years. 

    Up to thirty-two RS485 transceivers can occupy a single bus and can communicate at up to 10 Mb/s. It uses balanced, differential communications which provides much better noise immunity than RS232. RS485 is used where multiple transceivers must communicate and each transceiver is part of a multi-drop network. Devices are daisy-chained together, and each device has a unique address. It can also be used in 4 wire mode, where a master device driver can connect to the receivers of all slave devices and the slave drivers are connected to the master receiver.

    RS485 is used as the basis for many industrial data communications systems, including PROFIBUS. MODBUS based systems are also commonly implemented using RS485. Look up CANBUS, this is used in industry and in automotive vehicles.

    Where an RS485 bus is greater than 1200m long, repeaters may be used to extend the bus.

    I'm not gong to cover it here, but another option for long range communications is to use a 4-20mA current loop.

    Balanced v. Unbalanced

    In RS232, each signal appears on a single line with reference to a signal ground. When noise is picked up on a single signal line, it also shows with respect to ground. Once the noise level exceeds +/-3V, it may appear as a bit and errors will occur. This is an unbalanced signal line and although it has the advantage of simplicity, it is very limited in its usage.

    With balanced signals, two signal lines are dedicated per signal (A and B) and these lines carry opposite signals. When A is negative with respect to B, this is a binary one. When A is positive with respect to B, that is a binary zero.

    When noise occurs, it shows with the same polarity on each line and can be cancelled. The signal shows as a differential value between the two signal lines and is much easier to detect.

    Voltage Levels

    Voltage levels for RS485 are much smaller than those used for RS232.

    • +200mV DC to +6V DC is a logical zero
    • -200mV DC to -6V DC is a logical one

    Wiring

    In a RS485 system, both the transmit sides require 2 wires, a basic link will be 4 wires plus a reference earth. It is possible to bridge two wires of the driver and receiver such that the external wiring will work in 2 wire mode plus a reference earth. Be warned, some implementations on cheap interfaces are not isolated, I have fried computer equipment in the past!

    Cheap boards are never cheap!

    The additional cost of using screened cable is minimal, this is recommended. The screen is connected to earth at one side only. Typical cables are AWG24 or 0.5mm and there are a range of recommended cables. CAT5e STP may also be used, it is cheap and exceeds specifications.

    To allow 32 devices on a bus to both receive and transmit, there are some special considerations. The receiver of each node will always be connected to the bus and listening. The transmitter or driver can be transmitting a one, or a zero, or can be disabled from transmitting to the bus. It must be disabled when not in use, otherwise it would hold the bus at a one (idle state). As it can be in any one of three states, it is referred to as a tri-state device. Each driver has an enable line which must be active for the driver to connect to the bus. Many systems use the standard control signal RTS for this function. Where multiple systems share such a bus, each device must have a unique ID. We refer to this as a multi-drop bus. Once there are multiple nodes on a bus, the communications protocol must govern who speaks. This system will be half-duplex and a protocol like MODBUS will arbitrate who speaks.

    Simplex communications occur when a single device transmits and is received by a receiver.

    Duplex communications implies two separate signal paths, where each device can transmit and receive at the same time

    Terminators

    Every wire has physical properties which relate to its construction. One of these properties is called the characteristic impedance, the apparent impedance of the line to an AC signal. For good clean signal transmission, it is usually required to terminate a signal line at either end. The standards for RS485 specify a 120Ω resistor.

    Terminators can have disadvantages; they do add to the load that the node’s driver will experience.

    If the propagation delay on the line is less than one bit long, terminators are not necessary. For low speeds (<=38.4kb/s) termination is normally not required.

    Placement of terminators may be complex but normally they will be assigned at the end of a bus, two per bus.

    Some vendors recommend the use of AC terminators, where a resistor and a capacitor are used so that the line is terminated but does not cause a DC loading.

    Safety

    One of the issues we have when connecting two devices across a copper wire, it that the ground potential on the two sides may be different. This may cause there to be a common mode voltage difference between the two sides which may damage the equipment. The reason we have a reference earth connection is to tie all nodes to a common ground. The RS485 standard specifies this should not exceed -7VDC – +12VDC.

    Circumstances may exist when very large voltages are experienced on the nodes. In many cases, signals are optically isolated by optocouplers to prevent such high voltages doing damage. Where transients occur, these may be exceptionally high voltage and frequency. Protection for these include devices which break down under such circumstances. These are called shunts and there are a family of such devices.
